# Helping children prepare for a scientist conversation

[Home](../README.md) · [Official resource index](../INDEX.md)

A short preparation activity for parents and coaches helping children explore a **banana slug habitat or greenhouse idea**. The aim is to give children enough context to ask their own questions and use what they learn to develop their project.

## Give them a concrete starting point

**Our guest is a biologist and first-year Ph.D. student in the Graduate Group in Ecology at UC Davis, working with Dr. Simon Anthony.** Her research interests focus on **wildlife disease ecology**: how the environment, animals, and microbes interact in ways that affect health and the spread of disease.

More specifically, she is interested in **how environmental conditions influence microbial communities in bats**, and how those changes may affect disease transmission and susceptibility—how diseases spread and how likely an animal is to become infected or ill. She wants to combine **field work and laboratory research** to understand how environmental changes affect wildlife. These are her stated research interests; the conversation is an opportunity to ask what she is currently investigating and which methods she uses or hopes to use.

The connection to the children's banana slug project is **how an animal's surroundings affect its life and health, and how we can study that relationship**. The children want to protect slugs and monitor their lives. She can offer a scientific perspective on choosing observations, collecting evidence, and finding out whether a proposed habitat helps. Her stated research focuses on bats and disease ecology, so questions about particular slug care needs may need a specialist or further research.

The [Anthony Lab website](https://anthonylab.vetmed.ucdavis.edu/) provides broader background on the lab's wildlife virus research. Its [Virus Discovery page](https://anthonylab.vetmed.ucdavis.edu/virus-discovery) has bat and field-sampling photographs to explore together. Use these as conversation starters; the lab's other projects are not necessarily her own work.

## A one-minute introduction to read aloud

Here is a simple way to introduce her research to the children:

> “We’re meeting a scientist at UC Davis who is studying ecology—how living things and their surroundings are connected. She is interested in bats and the tiny microbes associated with them. Microbes include things like bacteria that are too small to see with just our eyes. Many are harmless or helpful.
>
> “She wants to understand how changes in the places bats live might affect their microbes and health. She is interested in investigating outdoors and using a laboratory to examine clues more closely. We can ask her what that looks like in her own work.
>
> “Our animal is a banana slug. We want to protect it and learn about its life. We can ask how a scientist would decide what to observe, what to measure, and how to find out whether our idea helps.”

A scientist's findings about bats do not automatically apply to slugs. Ask which ideas transfer and which need further research or a slug specialist.

## A 15-minute activity to generate questions

1. **Hear the introduction (2 minutes).** Ask the child to explain back one thing the scientist wants to learn. Explain only the words they need: ecology means studying living things and their relationships with their surroundings; field work means studying outside the laboratory.
2. **Look and wonder (3 minutes).** Open the lab's Virus Discovery page together and look at a bat or sampling photograph. Ask “What do you notice?” and “What do you wonder?” A photo can prompt questions about observation and tools; it is not an instruction to copy professional sampling.
3. **Connect to the child's idea (5 minutes).** Have the child draw a banana slug and its surroundings, or use the team's existing sketch. Ask “What do we want to learn about its day?”, “What would we watch or measure?”, and “What would tell us our design is helping?” Mark guesses with a question mark. Temperature, dampness, shelter, food, and activity are possible discussion topics, not a prescribed experiment or claims about the scientist's exact methods.
4. **Write three questions (5 minutes).** Let each child choose one about the scientist's work, one about how to investigate something, and one about the team's idea. Write their wording first. Help make a question clearer only after asking what they mean.

For each question, the child should be able to finish: “I want to know this because…” Avoid requiring specialist vocabulary or an impressive-sounding question.

## Optional question starters

Use these if a child is stuck; they are examples to adapt, not an assigned script. Keep room for questions that emerge from the scientist's introduction.

| Starting point | Example in a child's voice |
|---|---|
| Research question | What is one thing about bats that you are trying to find out? |
| Daily work | Could you tell us about something you have done, or hope to do, outdoors and then in the lab? |
| Evidence | How do you find out about something too small to see? |
| Environment | What changes in an animal's home are you interested in studying? |
| Observation | How do you watch an animal without changing what it normally does? |
| Monitoring | What should we measure if we want to learn about a banana slug's daily life? |
| Testing our idea | How could we find out whether our greenhouse helps the slugs? |
| Unintended effects | Could something we build to help an animal accidentally make its home worse? |
| Next step | What would you investigate first if you were starting our project? |

Ask for general scientific guidance where appropriate. The scientist can distinguish what they know from what needs a slug specialist or further research. A useful follow-up is “What clue would tell us that?”

## Preparing as a team

Each child can prepare three questions. Treat these as candidates rather than a checklist to finish during the meeting. Combine duplicates with the children's agreement, aim for one priority question per child, and keep the others as backups. In a 30–45-minute conversation, leave time for the scientist’s introduction, the children's project explanation, and spontaneous follow-ups.

A possible 35-minute outline is 5 minutes for introductions, 5 minutes for the children to explain their idea, 20 minutes for questions and discussion, and 5 minutes for next steps. Let the scientist and the coach adjust this.

The kids can explain their idea using three prompts:

- We chose banana slugs because…
- We hope our idea will help by…
- We still need to find out…

After the conversation, each child records one thing learned and one thing to change, investigate, or test. Keep a distinction between the scientist's advice, the children's interpretation, and evidence from a later test.
